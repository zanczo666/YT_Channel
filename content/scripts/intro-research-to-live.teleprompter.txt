Let me show you how an idea travels through my pipeline.
A pipeline is simply a fixed order of steps.
Every idea goes through the same steps, in the same order.
Step one is research.
I start with a published paper or a clear observation about the market.
I write it down as a hypothesis: one claim that a test can reject.
A hypothesis is not a strategy yet.
It's a question I want the market to answer.
Step two is the code.
A code generator turns the hypothesis into an Expert Advisor.
An Expert Advisor, or EA, is a trading program that runs inside MetaTrader.
Before any real test, the EA gets a simple check.
Does it place trades at all, and do they follow the rules?
An EA that never trades is usually a bug, not a careful strategy.
Step three is the backtest in MetaTrader five.
A backtest replays the strategy on historical prices.
It answers one question: what would have happened in the past?
That's useful, but it's also where most mistakes hide.
Step four is a second engine, written in Python.
I run the same logic outside MetaTrader, with my own code.
If the two engines disagree, something is wrong.
Sometimes the bug is in the strategy.
Sometimes it's in the tester itself.
Step five is validation.
Here the strategy goes through a series of gates.
Each gate asks one hard question.
Does it survive higher spread and slippage?
Those are the hidden costs between the price you see and the price you get.
Does it work on data it has never seen?
We call that out-of-sample data.
Is the result stable when I change the settings a little?
And how many versions did I try before this one looked good?
Each gate gives a verdict: pass, conditional, or fail.
One critical fail stops the whole review.
Step six is paper trading.
The EA trades live prices on a demo account, with no real money at risk.
Real spreads, real fills, real trading hours.
The market checks things a backtest never looked at.
Live trading is the last step.
Every update has to earn its way into production.
Any step can stop an idea.
When that happens, the reason goes into a log.
That log is the raw material for this channel.

Why should you care about my pipeline?
Because most backtests you see online tell one side of the story.
Profitable backtests are easy to sell.
I test whether they survive.
Here's the problem.
If you try enough ideas, one will look brilliant by chance.
Then you see that one, and never the many that failed before it.
This is called selection bias, and it fools experienced traders too.
The same thing happens inside one strategy.
Tune it long enough, and it learns the past by heart.
We call that overfitting.
The curve looks perfect on old data and breaks on new data.
So a failed test is not wasted work.
It tells you which assumption was wrong.
That's why I show failures with the same care as successes.
To be clear, a systematic approach does not promise more profit than manual trading.
Its advantage is simpler: you can measure it, and you can test it.

So how can you use this on your own strategies?
Before you trust any backtest, yours or someone else's, ask a few questions.
First: were real costs included?
Spread, commission and slippage can turn a rising curve into a flat one.
Second: was any data kept away from the building process?
If every candle was used for tuning, the result is a memory test.
Third: how many versions were tried before this one?
Many versions of one idea are still one idea, tested many times.
Fourth: does the result hold when the settings change a little?
If a small change kills it, the result was probably luck.
You don't need a big pipeline to ask these questions.
